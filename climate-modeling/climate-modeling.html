Climate Modeling: From Physics to Evidence

PHSCS 137 · Interactive tutorial · About 20 minutes

1. What is a climate model?

A climate model is a mathematical representation of processes that govern Earth's climate. Models use conservation of energy, mass, and momentum, along with approximations for processes too small or complex to resolve directly.

Weather versus climate: Weather describes conditions at a particular time and place. Climate describes statistical patterns, including averages, variability, and extremes, over longer periods. A climate model need not predict the weather on a particular day decades from now to estimate long-term climate responses.

2. Why are climate models different from one another?

Physical laws and numerical calculations

Models divide the Earth into cells or other computational elements, calculate energy and matter exchanges, and step forward in time. Finer resolution can represent more detail but requires more computing power.

Approximations and inputs

Clouds, turbulence, ice dynamics, and other processes require simplified descriptions called parameterizations. Models also need external forcings, such as changes in greenhouse gases, sunlight, and volcanic aerosols.

Different models may agree on broad trends while differing in regional details and feedback strengths. An ensemble compares multiple simulations to explore variability and uncertainty.

Additional greenhouse forcing describes how much greenhouse gases disturb Earth’s energy balance. Positive values represent added warming; negative values represent reduced greenhouse forcing relative to the baseline, such as removing excess atmospheric CO₂. Climate restoring response (λ) measures how strongly outgoing radiation and other climate processes respond to a temperature change. A larger positive λ means a smaller equilibrium temperature change for the same forcing. Units: W/(m²·K). This linearized model is centered on a reference climate of 15°C. Negative forcing is relative to the reference state; actual cooling depends on all forcings and the system’s adjustment over time.

5. Verification: Did we solve the equations correctly?

Verification checks the implementation of a mathematical model: coding, units, numerical methods, and whether the computer solves the specified equations correctly. A verified model can still omit important physics.

Other verification tests: conservation checks, dimensional consistency, testing known limiting cases, resolution-convergence tests, and comparisons against exact solutions where available.

6. Validation: Does the model represent the real climate?

Validation evaluates whether a model reproduces relevant observations sufficiently well for a particular purpose. It is not proof that every future prediction will be correct.
