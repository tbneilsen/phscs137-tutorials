Geothermal Energy: Heat from Beneath Earth's Surface

1. How can heat underground generate electricity?

Earth's interior is hot. In many regions, temperature increases with depth. The rate of increase is called the geothermal gradient, usually expressed in °C per kilometer. Hot rock can heat water circulating through underground wells or a heat exchanger. At the surface, some of that thermal energy can be converted into electrical energy.

Follow the energy

  1. Cooler fluid enters an injection well.
  2. The fluid absorbs thermal energy from hot rock.
  3. Heated fluid returns through a production well.
  4. A heat exchanger transfers heat to a power cycle, which drives a generator.
  5. The cooled geothermal fluid is circulated back underground.

Closed-loop concept: This tutorial represents a simplified closed fluid circuit. Real geothermal systems differ: some circulate fluid through fractures in rock, while others use sealed subsurface pipes. The actual heat transfer depends on geology, contact area, and time.

2. Why does location matter?

Some places have unusually hot rock near the surface because of volcanic activity or regional geology. Other places have lower geothermal gradients and require deeper drilling to reach similar temperatures.

Temperature at depth (°C) = surface temperature (°C) + gradient (°C/km) × depth (km)

Electrical power (MW) = thermal power (MW) × conversion efficiency

Model assumptions: Surface temperature = 15°C; fluid enters the subsurface at 30°C; returning fluid reaches 80% of the temperature difference between 30°C and the rock; fluid specific heat = 4.18 kJ/(kg·°C). The model assumes enough subsurface heat transfer to maintain that outlet temperature at the selected flow. It does not account for reservoir cooling, pumping energy, well losses, equipment limits, or economic feasibility. Conversion efficiency is independently adjustable; real achievable efficiency depends strongly on temperature.

4. What does a temperature gradient look like?

At the same depth, a higher gradient gives a higher estimated rock temperature. The relationship is approximately linear in this introductory model.
