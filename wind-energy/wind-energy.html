Wind Energy: Understanding a Turbine Power Curve

PHSCS 137 · Interactive tutorial · 15–20 minutes

1. How wind becomes electricity

Wind carries kinetic energy. Air moving past the blades creates forces that rotate the rotor. The shaft drives a generator, converting mechanical power into electrical power.

A turbine can extract only part of the energy passing through its rotor. Its controller also limits electrical output and shuts the machine down in extreme winds.

Rated power is the turbine's nominal maximum operating output—not the power it produces at every wind speed.

2. Why does wind speed matter?

For the same air density and rotor area, the power available in the wind increases with the cube of wind speed.

Pwind = ½ ρ A v³

ρ = air density (kg/m³), A = rotor swept area (m²), v = wind speed (m/s). This is power in the wind, not the turbine's electrical output.

The swept area is the circular area traced by the rotating blades. Larger rotors intercept more wind energy. Keep wind speed fixed and try doubling the area.

An illustrative value is 40% of the kinetic power passing through the rotor area converted into electrical power. This includes aerodynamic extraction and mechanical/electrical losses. The theoretical Betz limit is 59.3% for aerodynamic extraction alone. Actual performance varies with wind speed and turbine design.

What is cut-out speed? At approximately 30 m/s in this example, the wind is strong enough to risk excessive loads on the blades, tower, and drivetrain. The turbine controller stops electricity generation and brings the rotor to a safe state to protect the equipment. This is why the curve drops abruptly to zero even though the wind still contains substantial kinetic energy. Turbines typically restart only after conditions return to an acceptable operating range.

Below cut-in: no generation. Between cut-in and rated: rapidly rising output. Between rated and cut-out: controlled output near 1 MW (slightly declining in the supplied graph). At cut-out: protective shutdown.
