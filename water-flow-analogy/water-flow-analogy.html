Water Pipes: Capacity vs. Actual Flow

Guided interactive tutorial · Imagine a water supply feeding a main pipe that splits into five branches. Each branch supplies a different use. Opening a valve adds demand; closing it removes demand. The main pipe can supply up to 350 L/min, but it does not always carry 350 L/min.

1. Open and close valves

Check a branch to open its valve. The flow rates are simplified fixed demands.

2. Water available in the tank

Assume a tank holds 700 liters of usable water. The flow rate determines how quickly it empties.

3. Make predictions, then test them

Try these experiments: Open only the garden hose. Then add the sink. Next open all branches. Observe the total requested flow and whether the system can meet it.

Question 1. Only the 40 L/min garden hose is open. How much water flows in the main pipe?
Question 2. The sink (80 L/min) and shower (120 L/min) are open. What is the total flow?
Question 3. What happens if all five valves are opened at once?
Question 4. With a 700 L tank and a total flow of 100 L/min, how long does the water last (assuming no refill)?

4. Explain the analogy in your own words

Before returning to Learning Suite, answer: Why does a main pipe capable of 350 L/min deliver only 40 L/min when just the garden hose is open? What determines how long the water tank lasts?

For classroom discussion: the tank's total water is analogous to stored energy; flow rate is analogous to power; maximum supply is analogous to a power station's maximum output rating. Real plumbing flow also depends on pressure and resistance, which this model intentionally simplifies.
