How Do Future Societies Shape Climate Predictions?

Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) · PHSCS 137 · Interactive tutorial · About 20 minutes

1. A climate model needs assumptions about the future

Climate models use physical laws to calculate how the climate responds to changes in greenhouse gases, aerosols, and other influences. But the model cannot know future choices about energy, technology, land use, or policy. Scientists therefore study scenarios—plausible, internally consistent descriptions of different futures, not forecasts of what people will definitely do.

Key distinction: An SSP describes a socioeconomic future. An SSP-based emissions scenario supplies a possible pathway of climate-changing emissions and concentrations. A climate model then calculates the physical response.

2. Five socioeconomic storylines

SSP1 — Sustainability

Cooperation, lower resource intensity, investments in health, education, and cleaner technologies.

SSP2 — Middle of the Road

Development follows broadly historical patterns; progress is uneven.

SSP3 — Regional Rivalry

Fragmented regions, slower development and technological progress, greater challenges to adaptation and mitigation.

SSP4 — Inequality

Large differences in wealth, opportunities, and ability to adapt within and among societies.

SSP5 — Fossil-fueled Development

Rapid economic growth and energy-intensive lifestyles with strong reliance on fossil fuels unless policy changes.

An SSP storyline alone does not specify a unique emissions or temperature trajectory. Different policies and assumptions can produce different emissions pathways within the same socioeconomic storyline.

Understanding the second number in an SSP scenario

An SSP number (such as SSP1, SSP2, or SSP5) identifies a possible future socioeconomic storyline. The number after the dash describes the approximate total radiative forcing reached in the year 2100, relative to preindustrial conditions, in watts per square meter (W/m²).

For example, SSP2-4.5 combines the SSP2 “Middle of the Road” storyline with a pathway reaching approximately 4.5 W/m² of radiative forcing by 2100. The 4.5 is not a temperature increase in °C, an atmospheric CO₂ concentration, or an annual emissions rate.

Radiative forcing is a change in Earth’s net energy balance (incoming minus outgoing radiation). Positive forcing tends to warm Earth; negative forcing tends to cool it.

An SSP storyline can be combined with different emissions and mitigation assumptions. The number after the dash specifies the forcing level associated with the particular scenario, not an inevitable outcome.

The shaded interval is a very likely assessed range, not an error bar on the socioeconomic storyline.

Why are there ranges? Different climate models respond differently to the same forcing, and natural variability and uncertain physical feedbacks also affect projections. Scenario uncertainty concerns future human choices; climate-response uncertainty concerns the physical response under a given scenario.

6. Scenario or prediction?

Consider the statement: “Global temperature will rise by exactly 2.7°C by 2100.” Is that what SSP2-4.5 tells us?

What the scenario specifies

A possible pathway of future greenhouse gas emissions, atmospheric concentrations, and radiative forcing, based on assumptions about society and policy.

What the climate model estimates

Climate responses such as warming, precipitation, and sea-level change under that pathway, with uncertainties. A projection is conditional: if this scenario occurs, these outcomes are plausible.

SSP2-4.5 has an assessed best estimate of 2.7°C warming for 2081–2100, not a guaranteed temperature in the calendar year 2100.
