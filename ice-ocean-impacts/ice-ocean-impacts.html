Global Warming: Ice, Sea Level, and Ocean Chemistry

PHSCS 137 · Interactive tutorial · Approximately 25 minutes · Predict, experiment, explain

1. Why does Arctic sea ice matter?

Sea ice is frozen ocean water that floats. Bright ice reflects much of the incoming sunlight; darker open ocean absorbs more. When sea ice retreats, additional solar energy can be absorbed, contributing to further warming. This is called the ice–albedo feedback.

2. Floating ice versus land ice

Floating sea ice already displaces water, so melting it causes little direct change in sea level. By contrast, melting ice on land (such as glaciers and the Greenland ice sheet) adds water to the ocean, raising sea level. Sea-ice loss still matters greatly for albedo and Arctic ecosystems.

3. Ocean warming: the rate of heat transfer into water

The ocean absorbs most of the excess heat accumulating in Earth's climate system. Because water has a high specific heat capacity, raising its temperature requires substantial energy. The average power needed also depends on how quickly that warming occurs.

Q = mcΔT   and   P = Q / Δt = mcΔT / Δt

Power is the rate of energy transfer. For the same mass and temperature increase, shorter heating time requires greater average power.

Use c = 4180 J/(kg·°C), an approximate value for liquid water. This is a sample volume of water, not a whole-ocean heat-content estimate.

4. Two major causes of sea-level rise

Thermal expansion: as seawater warms, its volume generally increases. Land-ice melt: melting glaciers and ice sheets transfers additional water to the oceans. Both processes contribute to global mean sea-level rise.

Ocean warming does not occur only at the surface. Heat can be transported into deeper water. Warming a thicker layer causes more thermal expansion and greater sea-level rise.

5. How does excess atmospheric CO₂ affect ocean pH?

As atmospheric CO₂ increases, more CO₂ dissolves in surface seawater. The resulting chemical reactions increase hydrogen-ion concentration and lower pH. The ocean is still generally alkaline (pH above 7), but it becomes less alkaline.

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

6. How does ocean pH affect shell development?

Many marine organisms build shells or skeletons from calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). When more dissolved CO₂ shifts seawater carbonate chemistry, fewer carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) are generally available for calcification. Lower carbonate saturation can make shell growth more difficult and can increase dissolution risk.

Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ CaCO₃ (shell material)
