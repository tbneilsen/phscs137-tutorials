Light, Molecules, and the Greenhouse Effect

1. All electromagnetic radiation is light

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all electromagnetic waves. They differ in wavelength (the distance between successive crests), frequency (cycles per second), and photon energy. Move the wavelength slider to explore.

2. Why do the Sun and Earth emit different kinds of light?

Warm objects emit a range of wavelengths. Hotter objects emit more radiation and their emission peaks at shorter wavelengths. The Sun (~5800 K) emits strongly in visible and nearby wavelengths, while Earth (~288 K) emits mostly infrared radiation.

Sun (~5800 K)Earth (~288 K)

Peak wavelength (µm) ≈ 2900 ÷ temperature (K)   (Wien’s law)

Total radiated power: P = eσAT4   (Stefan–Boltzmann law)

Here P is the total power radiated (W), e is emissivity (0–1), A is radiating surface area (m²), T is absolute temperature (K), and σ = 5.67 × 10−8 W/(m²·K4) is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. For a blackbody, e = 1.

2A. Why do neon lights glow different colors?

Atoms have discrete electronic energy levels. In a gas-discharge lamp, energetic electrons collide with gas atoms and excite them. When the atoms return to lower energy levels, they emit photons at characteristic wavelengths. Choose a gas to compare its emission lines.

Photon energy = difference between energy levels: ΔE = hf = hc/λ

Connection to greenhouse gases: Atoms can also absorb photons that match allowed transitions. Greenhouse gases are molecules, however, and their important infrared absorption usually involves vibrational and rotational energy transitions rather than the electronic transitions highlighted by neon lamps.

These are emission spectra, not absorption spectra. Line intensities depend on lamp conditions and are not to scale. Some gases also emit strongly outside the visible range.

3. Why does one molecule absorb a wavelength that another does not?

Molecules have particular ways to rotate and vibrate. A photon can be absorbed when its energy matches an allowed change in molecular energy. For a vibrational transition to absorb infrared light, the vibration must also change the molecule's electric dipole moment. Thus, molecular structure determines which infrared wavelengths are absorbed.

Nitrogen (N₂) and oxygen (O₂) make up most of the atmosphere, but their symmetric molecules have no strong ordinary infrared-active vibrational absorption. Water vapor, CO₂, methane, and ozone absorb infrared in different spectral regions.

5. Sunlight comes in; infrared radiation goes out

Much incoming sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The surface emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb at particular infrared wavelengths and emit infrared radiation in different directions, including back toward the surface. Some infrared radiation escapes directly through relatively transparent spectral regions.

Schematic energy pathways, not a scale drawing. Greenhouse gases do not create energy; they change how energy moves through the atmosphere and the temperatures needed for energy balance.

6. Which infrared wavelengths can escape?

Choose a gas and adjust its atmospheric concentration relative to a reference level. Compare the resulting spectral transmission. A setting of 100% represents the reference concentration for the selected gas; 200% represents twice that concentration. This is not the percentage of air made up by that gas. Low transmission means that less surface infrared at that wavelength travels directly through the atmosphere without absorption; it does not mean that no energy ultimately escapes to space.

Incoming solar energy absorbed = outgoing radiation to space at equilibrium

Increasing greenhouse-gas absorption initially changes how much infrared escapes. The Earth–atmosphere system can then warm until outgoing radiation once again balances absorbed sunlight. The final surface temperature can be higher even though total energy is conserved.
