How Hybrid Electric Cars Work

1. A hybrid has two ways to drive its wheels

A conventional hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) combines a gasoline engine, an electric motor/generator, and a relatively small battery. The engine can power the wheels and can also help charge the battery. The electric motor can drive the wheels, and during braking it can act as a generator.

Conventional hybrids do not need a wall plug. Their batteries receive energy from regenerative braking and, when necessary, the gasoline engine. A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) has a larger externally rechargeable battery.

2. Where does the energy go?

Energy is conserved, but only a fraction of gasoline's chemical energy becomes useful wheel energy. Hybrid systems can reduce losses by shutting off the engine when appropriate, operating it more efficiently, and recovering some energy while braking.

Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ input energy

A battery does not create energy. In a conventional hybrid, electricity in the battery ultimately comes from gasoline or recovered vehicle kinetic energy.

4. Experiment with regenerative braking

When a car slows, it loses kinetic energy. In a conventional car, most braking energy becomes heat. A hybrid's motor/generator can convert some of the lost kinetic energy back into stored battery energy.

6. HEV versus PHEV versus BEV

Conventional hybrid (HEV)

Small battery, gasoline engine, regenerative braking. No plug required. Gasoline is the external energy source.

Plug-in hybrid (PHEV)

Larger battery charged from the grid; can drive some distance electrically, then use gasoline.

Battery-electric vehicle (BEV)

Large battery charged from the grid, electric motor, no gasoline engine.

A plug-in hybrid is not the same as a conventional hybrid. Both can use regenerative braking, but only the plug-in hybrid normally accepts external charging.
