Methane and Carbon Dioxide: Two Greenhouse Gas Cycles

1. Where do CO₂ and methane come from, and where do they go?

Both gases contain carbon, but their atmospheric removal pathways differ. CO₂ exchanges among the atmosphere, plants, soils, ocean, and rocks. Methane (CH₄) is released by wetlands, livestock, landfills, fossil-fuel operations, and other sources; most atmospheric methane is chemically destroyed by reactions involving the hydroxyl radical (OH), ultimately producing CO₂ and water.

Methane cycle

Key distinction: Methane is removed from the atmosphere relatively quickly through chemical reactions, primarily oxidation initiated by hydroxyl (OH) radicals, whereas CO₂ has no comparable rapid chemical removal process. Methane has an approximate atmospheric lifetime of 12 years, but its oxidation creates CO₂, so the carbon does not simply disappear. CO₂ is transferred from the atmosphere into plants through photosynthesis, into soils through organic matter and carbon storage, into oceans through gas exchange and dissolution, and eventually into rocks through weathering, sedimentation, and carbonate formation.

2. Compare what happens when emissions continue

Think of atmospheric abundance as a bank account: emissions are deposits and removal is withdrawals. Methane has a relatively fast chemical removal pathway; CO₂ is redistributed among reservoirs, with some excess persisting much longer.

Change in atmospheric amount = emissions − removal

These two gases are not interchangeable: the same mass of emissions does not produce the same warming, and their long-term behavior differs.

Reducing methane emissions does not immediately remove methane already in the atmosphere. Instead, atmospheric methane gradually decreases as chemical reactions remove existing methane faster than new methane is added. Eventually, the atmosphere approaches a new steady-state concentration.

Relative CH₄ abundance = new emissions fraction + (1 − new emissions fraction) × e−t/12 yr

Reducing methane emissions can cause atmospheric methane to decrease within decades. In contrast, stabilizing atmospheric CO₂ generally requires global net CO₂ emissions to approach zero, because a substantial portion of emitted CO₂ accumulates in the atmosphere.

5. Why do graphs use CO₂-equivalent units?

A kilogram of methane and a kilogram of CO₂ do not have the same warming effect. Global warming potential (GWP) compares the time-integrated climate influence of a pulse of each gas over a specified number of years, relative to CO₂. Multiplying emissions by GWP gives CO₂-equivalent emissions (CO₂e).

CO₂e (kg) = emissions (kg of gas) × GWP over chosen time horizon

Methane has a Global Warming Potential (GWP) of approximately 80 over 20 years and 28 over 100 years. The time horizon determines how its warming effect is compared with the same mass of CO₂.

For this educational comparison, GWP20 = 80 and GWP100 = 28 for methane (rounded illustrative factors; published values vary with assessment and whether fossil methane is distinguished). CO₂ has GWP = 1.
